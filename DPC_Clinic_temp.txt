Background of Medicine 3.1 History of the US Healthcare system  For much of the early twentieth century, healthcare in the United States was delivered largely through independent, physician-owned practices and community-based hospitals. Care was predominantly paid for directly by patients, and clinical decision-making was guided primarily by physician judgment and patient need rather than external financial structures.
The introduction and widespread adoption of employer-sponsored health insurance following World War II marked a pivotal turning point. Employer-sponsored health insurance emerged as a response to federally imposed wage controls, allowing employers to attract and retain workers by offering health benefits in place of higher wages. As insurance coverage expanded, third-party payers became the primary mechanism through which healthcare services were financed. While this model improved access to care for many Americans, it also fundamentally changed the relationship between patients, physicians, and the cost of care. Patients became insulated from the true price of services, while physicians and hospitals became increasingly dependent on payer reimbursement structures to remain financially viable.
Over time, reimbursement models grew more complex and increasingly emphasized billing codes, documentation requirements, and utilization metrics. Healthcare delivery began to shift toward volume-based incentives, rewarding the quantity of services provided rather than the quality or effectiveness of care. As a result, healthcare organizations adapted by optimizing throughput, expanding service lines with higher reimbursement potential, and investing heavily in administrative infrastructure to manage billing, compliance, and payer negotiations.
The increasing role of profit-driven entities, including insurance companies, pharmaceutical benefit managers (PBM), private equity firms, and large healthcare systems, further moved this transformation. Financial performance became the only driver of organizational decision-making, influencing staffing models, appointment lengths, referral patterns, and care pathways. While profitability and sustainability are necessary for any system to function (should healthcare be a for profit endeavor??), the growing prioritization of financial metrics in healthcare has occurred at the expense of care coordination, preventative medicine, and long-term patient relationships.
These incentives have contributed to consolidation across the healthcare landscape, with independent practices and hospitals increasingly absorbed into large systems. Although consolidation has produced efficiencies in some areas, it has also distanced clinical decision-making from frontline care and reduced physician autonomy. Compensation models tied to productivity and relative value units (RVUs) have reinforced high-volume practice patterns, further compressing visit times and limiting opportunities for meaningful patient engagement.
In the current system, profit and productivity incentives are deeply embedded at nearly every level of healthcare delivery. Primary care, which historically functioned as the cornerstone of preventative and coordinated care, has been impacted due to comparatively lower reimbursement rates. This misalignment has led to underinvestment in primary care, increased reliance on downstream services, and a system that is more reactive than preventative in nature.
The current healthcare system suffers from a fundamental misalignment: its focus on financial incentives has undermined patient outcomes, creating a model that rewards volume and complexity over prevention and continuity. As is often the case in the United States, money drives decisions, not health. This reality underscores the need for alternative care models that realign incentives to prioritize patient outcomes rather than shareholder value.  3.2 Problems in Modern Primary Care Today, primary care physicians often manage patient panels of 2,000 to 3,500 individuals, far exceeding what is feasible for meaningful, relationship-based care. Combined with average visit times of only seven to twelve minutes, physicians are left with limited opportunity to address preventative care, manage chronic conditions effectively, or engage in comprehensive patient counseling. These pressures are compounded by administrative burdens, physicians spend nearly half of their workday on non-clinical tasks such as documentation, prior authorizations, and billing2 which contribute to burnout, job dissatisfaction, and high turnover, further disrupting continuity of care.
The current fee-for-service reimbursement model reinforces these structural challenges. By rewarding volume and procedural interventions over prevention and outcomes, the system incentivizing more visits, tests, and referrals. As a result, primary care is often reactive rather than proactive, focusing on acute problems rather than long-term health maintenance. Care coordination is further complicated by fragmented health systems, siloed electronic records, and multiple insurers, leading to duplicative testing, delayed follow-up, and inconsistent management of chronic conditions.
Access is another critical challenge. High deductibles, co-pays, and insurance complexity create financial barriers for many patients, particularly those who are uninsured or underinsured. These obstacles often delay routine care, resulting in worsened chronic disease outcomes and increased preventable emergency department visits. 
Collectively, these challenges demonstrate that modern primary care, as currently structured, is constrained by misaligned incentives, administrative burden, and limited time for patient engagement. A new approach one like Direct Primary Care, that prioritize smaller patient panels, longer visits, and a focus on outcomes rather than volume, will flip the incentives around. Placing preventative medicine as the goal, not increased shareholder value.
3.3 Consequences for Patients and Physicians Patients face limited access, rushed appointments, fragmented care, and higher out-of-pocket costs, which contribute to delayed diagnoses, poorly managed chronic conditions, and increased preventable hospital visits. For physicians, the high patient volumes, administrative burdens, and productivity-driven incentives lead to burnout, reduced job satisfaction, and limited professional autonomy. Together, these consequences perpetuate a cycle of poor outcomes, strained relationships, and a system that prioritizes volume over health.
3.4 Why Change is Needed Now The U.S. healthcare system is under unprecedented strain, making change urgent. Patients are increasingly frustrated with insurance complexity, high out-of-pocket costs, and opaque pricing, which remove trust and limit access to care. Rising medical costs are creating financial pressure for both families and employers, who are seeking more predictable, value-driven alternatives. Meanwhile, the primary care workforce continues to shrink, contributing to longer wait times, overextended physicians, and reduced continuity of care. These challenges, rising costs, workforce shortages, and misaligned incentives, give a place for new care models that improve access, control spending, and realign incentives around patient outcomes.
 



 
Direct Primary Care: The Solution 4.1 What is the DPC Model? Direct Primary Care (DPC) is a healthcare delivery model designed to restore the patient–physician relationship by removing insurance from the primary care equation. Under this model, patients pay a predictable monthly membership fee directly to the clinic in exchange for comprehensive primary care services. This fee will cover office visits, extended appointments, preventive care, chronic disease management, and direct communication with the physician through phone, text, or telemedicine. By eliminating third-party billing, DPC significantly reduces administrative complexity, allowing physicians to focus their time and resources on patient care rather than documentation and reimbursement requirements.
The DPC model enables physicians to maintain substantially smaller patient panels, as reimbursement isn’t relied on as the only way the clinic/physician gets paid. This structure allows for longer visits, proactive care, and deeper continuity, which are essential for effective prevention and chronic disease management. From an operational standpoint, DPC practices benefit from lower overhead, predictable recurring revenue, and simplified staffing needs, creating a financially sustainable model without sacrificing quality. This model looks to align the goals of what healthcare should be, improving patient outcomes, not increasing shareholder profit. 
4.2 Evidence and Data Supporting Direct Primary Care A growing body of evidence supports DPC as an effective and financially sustainable alternative to traditional fee-for-service primary care. Primary care delivered through a DPC model has demonstrated the ability to lower total healthcare costs while improving patient outcomes and provider experience3. By removing primary care from insurance billing and aligning financial incentives directly between patients and physicians, DPC addresses many of the inefficiencies that drive unnecessary utilization and rising costs.
Multiple employer-sponsored and population-based DPC programs have documented significant reductions in high-cost care utilization. These include substantial decreases in emergency department visits and hospitalizations, driven by improved access to care, longer visits, and earlier intervention. The article highlights employer data showing reductions of up to 54% in emergency room claims and 25% fewer hospital admissions among DPC participants, along with an overall reduction in total healthcare spending3. These findings suggest that when patients can access their physician easily and consistently, care shifts away from reactive, high-cost settings toward prevention with a vision of the long-term health goals of the patient.
DPC has also been associated with decreased reliance on specialist referrals. With smaller patient panels and extended appointment times, physicians are better equipped to manage complex and chronic conditions within the primary care setting. This improves care continuity and reduces unnecessary referrals, testing, and fragmented care. The DPC model supports proactive follow-up and personalized treatment plans, which are particularly effective in managing chronic diseases that account for most of the healthcare spending in the United States3. Patient satisfaction is consistently higher in DPC practices compared to traditional primary care environments. Patients report improved access, stronger relationships with their physicians, and greater trust in their care plans3.
These relational improvements translate into more effective chronic disease management, better adherence to treatment recommendations, and better long-term outcomes. Importantly, the predictable, transparent pricing structure of DPC reduces financial barriers to care, particularly for uninsured patients and those with high-deductible health plans, further improving the model.
This demonstrates that DPC is not only clinically effective but also economically viable. By lowering administrative overhead, reducing unnecessary utilization, and prioritizing prevention and continuity, DPC supports the Quadruple Aim of healthcare: improved patient experience, better outcomes, lower costs, and improved provider well-being.  4.3 Why the DPC Model Works
 The Direct Primary Care model works because it aligns the incentives of the physician and the patient around a shared objective: maintaining health rather than reacting to illness. In a DPC practice, the physician’s workload and professional satisfaction improve when patients are healthy, stable, and well-managed. Unlike traditional models that reward frequent visits, high volume, and complex billing, DPC financially incentivizes physicians to prevent disease progression, reduce unnecessary utilization, and keep patients out of the clinic whenever possible. The most efficient and sustainable outcome for both parties is a healthy patient at home, not repeated visits driven by unmanaged conditions.
This incentive alignment fundamentally changes patient behavior as well. By removing copays, deductibles, and per-visit financial barriers, patients are encouraged to contact their primary care physician early, when symptoms first arise rather than delaying care until problems escalate. Patients no longer must weigh the cost of a visit against their health, making proactive engagement the default behavior. Earlier access enables physicians to intervene sooner, address lifestyle factors, adjust treatment plans, and prevent minor issues from developing into costly and complex conditions.
This structure represents a clear departure from the insurance-driven, fee-for-service system, where physicians are rewarded for throughput, documentation, and utilization rather than outcomes. In the current model, financial incentives across much of the healthcare system are tied to ongoing treatment and service utilization, often rewarding prolonged disease management rather than actual health. Patients are kept functional enough to remain productive and afford the next treatment yet rarely supported in achieving true long-term wellness. Direct Primary Care flips this dynamic at the primary care level by enabling smaller patient panels, longer visits, and direct, ongoing communication. This allows physicians to focus on understanding the full context of a patient’s health rather than optimizing care delivery around billing efficiency and revenue generation.
As a result, preventive care becomes the most rational and efficient approach for both patients and providers. Patients are motivated to seek care earlier and more consistently for preventive and maintenance visits, while physicians are incentivized to invest time upfront to stabilize and control health conditions before they progress. This alignment creates a reinforcing feedback loop that leads to fewer avoidable complications, reduced emergency department utilization, and more effective chronic disease management. The DPC model succeeds not by adding complexity, but by removing misaligned incentives and restoring a healthcare environment where keeping patients healthy is the most sustainable and economically rational outcome for all, patients and providers.

Services Offered & Operational Model
6.1 Core Services Onevia delivers comprehensive primary care through an all-inclusive membership model designed to eliminate financial barriers to access and encourage proactive engagement with healthcare. Membership includes unlimited primary care visits, extended appointment times of up to one-hour, unlimited urgent care access, unlimited telemedicine, annual wellness exams, and routine preventive services without copays, deductibles, or per-visit fees. Patients can walk in same day, receive care, and walk out entirely without paying anything, allowing them to focus entirely on their health rather than cost. This structure encourages patients to seek care early and often, knowing that access is not financially penalized, and supports early intervention and preventive medicine.
Annual and preventative laboratory testing is included as part of routine care when clinically appropriate, allowing for consistent monitoring of key health indicators and early identification of risk factors. Telemedicine is fully integrated into the care model and included in the membership, enabling patients to communicate with their physician through virtual visits, secure messaging, and remote follow-up without additional charges. After-hours access via text or phone is also included, ensuring timely guidance and continuity of care outside of traditional office hours.
6.2 Add-On & Additional Services In addition to core membership services, Onevia offers access to a range of additional services provided strictly at cost, with no additional markup or profit margin. This structure ensures that clinical decisions are not influenced by revenue incentives and that patients receive necessary services at the lowest possible price. There is no incentive for the physician to add on additional tests. By separating clinic revenue from ancillary utilization, the model maintains transparency and reinforces trust between patients and providers.
Additional services may include advanced laboratory testing, diagnostic imaging (X-ray, CT), medications (In-house pharmacy), vaccines, and select in-office procedures that fall outside routine primary care coverage. These services are offered at the same price the clinic pays to laboratories, pharmacies, or suppliers, allowing patients to access care at wholesale or near-wholesale rates. Whenever possible, pricing is disclosed upfront so patients can make informed decisions without concern for hidden fees or unexpected bills. We can provide them a menu in a sense of what services we offer and how much those cost. Again, something unheard of in medicine.
This approach mirrors a membership-based wholesale model (think Costco), in which financial sustainability is achieved through predictable subscription revenue rather than margins on products or services. By eliminating profit incentives tied to testing, prescriptions, or procedures, Onevia ensures that recommendations are driven solely by clinical need. Patients benefit from significantly lower out-of-pocket costs, while physicians remain focused on prevention, outcomes, and long-term health rather than utilization. All working to maintain the proper alignment of incentives, healthy patients.
As the clinic grows, this cost-based structure allows for the thoughtful addition of further additional services while preserving the integrity of the model. Pharmacy, Dental, and Vision will be discussed later.
6.3 Membership Pricing Model Onevia utilizes a straightforward membership pricing structure. Membership revenue serves as the primary money maker of the practice, allowing care decisions to remain independent of visit volume or additional service utilization.
Individual memberships are offered to adult patients seeking comprehensive primary care with unlimited access, extended visits, and telemedicine included. Family memberships bundle care for multiple household members under a single plan, reducing per-person cost while encouraging preventative care for all family members. This structure supports long-term continuity of care and lowers barriers for families to engage with primary care early and often.
Small and medium-sized business memberships are designed for employers seeking a predictable, cost-effective primary care benefit for their workforce. Employer plans provide employees with full access to the clinic while allowing businesses to control healthcare spending and reduce downstream costs associated with emergency care, absenteeism, and unmanaged chronic disease. These plans can be offered as a standalone benefit or paired with high-deductible or catastrophic insurance coverage.

Because additional services are provided at cost with no markup, membership fees represent the primary and predictable revenue stream. Operating costs scale primarily with staffing and space rather than visit volume, supporting strong margins once the target panel size is reached.
As the clinic grows, revenue scales linearly with membership while maintaining quality controls through panel caps. Additional physicians, locations, or employer contracts can be added incrementally, allowing Onevia to expand in a controlled and repeatable manner without compromising access or outcomes. This predictable, subscription-based revenue model forms the financial foundation for long-term sustainability and expansion.

 7.3 Daily Workflow
Scheduling is flexible, with appointment blocks ranging from 30 to 60 minutes depending on visit type and patient need. Preventive visits, annual exams, and complex or chronic disease visits will be scheduled for longer time blocks, while acute concerns and follow-ups may be addressed in shorter visits or through telemedicine. Same-day and next-day appointments are routinely available, allowing patients to be seen quickly when issues arise rather than waiting weeks for care.
Patients are encouraged to contact the clinic as soon as concerns develop. Communication occurs through secure messaging, phone calls, or telemedicine, with clinical staff triaging requests throughout the day. Many issues can be resolved through messaging or virtual visits, while patients who require in-person evaluation are brought into the office promptly, often the same day. Because the clinic does not rely on insurance billing or rigid scheduling templates, physicians maintain control over their schedules and can adjust availability to meet patient demand.
Chronic disease management is integrated into the daily workflow through scheduled check-ins, proactive outreach, and remote monitoring when appropriate. Patients with ongoing conditions are followed at regular intervals, with care plans adjusted before problems escalate. Time is also intentionally reserved each day for documentation, care coordination, and patient follow-up. Because documentation is focused on clinical care rather than billing requirements, administrative work is streamlined and completed efficiently, supporting both high-quality care and provider sustainability.

As patient enrollment approaches panel thresholds, additional physicians are added, allowing revenue to scale ahead of cost increases. This disciplined approach reduces execution risk and avoids overbuilding infrastructure ahead of demand.
Finally, the clinic’s market position is reinforced by its ability to evolve over time. While the core DPC offering is financially sustainable on its own, the clinic is structured to support future expansion into complementary services such as in-house pharmacy, dental, or vision care. These services use the existing patient base and operational platform, providing optional upside without compromising the integrity of the primary care model. By remaining flexible and patient-centered, the clinic is positioned not only to meet current market demand but to remain viable as healthcare delivery continues to shift toward preventative, and more value-driven models.
12.3 Implementation Timeline
The initial phase focuses on formation and preparation. During this period, the PLLC, MSO, and real estate entities are finalized, governing and management agreements are written, and financing for the clinic space is secured. Facility buildout is completed to support two physicians at launch while being sized for future expansion. Core systems, including the EMR, scheduling, patient communications, and basic administrative workflows, are implemented. Concurrently, branding, website launch, and early community outreach begin to support patient acquisition ahead of opening.
The second phase marks clinic launch and early operations. The clinic opens with two physicians and a lean support team, including medical assistants and operations staff. Patient onboarding begins immediately. During this phase, workflows are refined, access standards are established, and care delivery processes are optimized to support small patient panels and a preventative care focus. Patient enrollment is expected to grow steadily during this period, with close monitoring of access, quality, and operational performance.
The third phase begins as patient enrollment approaches operating breakeven. Once the clinic reaches financial stability, operating reserves are built and internal processes are standardized. Continued patient growth during this phase allows the clinic to operate with increasing margin while maintaining care quality. Importantly, no expansion in provider count occurs until enrollment and demand clearly justify additional capacity.
The final phase transitions the clinic toward scaled operations and expansion readiness. As patient enrollment approaches panel capacity for the initial two physicians, recruitment planning for additional providers begins. Expansion to three and then four physicians occur incrementally, with staffing, systems, and facilities already designed to accommodate growth without disruption. Because expansion is funded by recurring membership revenue rather than speculative capital, each step is executed conservatively and sustainably.










Risk and Risk Mitigation 13.1 Patient Acquisition Risk
As with any membership-based healthcare model, early patient acquisition is critical. Slower-than-expected enrollment will delay breakeven and extend the recovery period.
Mitigation: The clinic launches with me having a previous patient panel (from residency), providing an immediate patient base and community credibility. Small panel sizes, transparent pricing, and strong word-of-mouth dynamics inherent to DPC practices reduce reliance on paid marketing. The phased launch model also helps too, beginning with two physicians and expanding only as enrollment justifies, ensures costs remain aligned with demand.
13.2 Physician Burnout and Retention Risk
Physician burnout is a risk in primary care and can disrupt continuity of care and clinic growth.
Mitigation: The DPC model directly addresses burnout drivers by limiting panel sizes, eliminating insurance-driven administrative burden, and aligning compensation with patient retention and long-term health outcomes rather than visit volume. Physicians benefit from more manageable workloads, greater professional autonomy, and a practice environment designed for sustainability rather than throughput.
13.3 Financial and Cost-Control Risk Unexpected increases in staffing, facility, or operating costs could compress margins.
Mitigation: The clinic’s cost structure is intentionally lean, with fixed all-in rent, minimal administrative staffing, and limited clinical overhead. The separation of clinical operations, management services, and real estate ownership provides flexibility to adjust cost allocation without destabilizing the core practice. Founder compensation deferral during early operations further reduces fixed costs during the highest-risk period.
13.4 Regulatory and Compliance Risk
Healthcare operations involve regulatory complexity at the state and federal level, especially when expanding into other services.
Mitigation: The clinic’s structure isolates clinical decision-making within the PLLC while centralizing non-clinical services within the MSO, consistent with common healthcare compliance frameworks. Expansion into pharmacy, dental, or vision services is deferred until operational stability is achieved and will be pursued with appropriate licensing, legal review, and staged implementation. 13.5 Market Competition Risk Increased adoption of DPC or alternative care models could increase local competition.
Mitigation: The clinic differentiates itself through physician accessibility, preventative focus, and continuity of care. Small panel sizes and high patient engagement create strong retention dynamics that are difficult to replicate at scale. Because growth is demand-driven rather than volume-driven, competitive pressure does not require overextension or price competition.
